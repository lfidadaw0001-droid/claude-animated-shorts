# "The Bell That Broke Itself" — original script v1 (2026-11-03)
Format: cartoon-3d-short (~60-67 s, ~3.5 words/s), voice-00 steady narration, our own characters + story, no captions.
Facts: Great Bell of Westminster ("Big Ben"), Palace of Westminster, London, 1858-1859; cast at Whitechapel Bell Foundry (13.5 long tons / ~13.7 tonnes) on 10 April 1858, hauled 200 ft up the clock tower shaft; began striking 11 July 1859; amateur horologist Edmund Beckett Denison (later Lord Grimthorpe) insisted on an iron hammer weighing 6.5 cwt (~700 lb), more than twice the maximum weight recommended by the foundry; in September 1859 the bell developed a ~4 ft crack; after 3 years silent, Sir George Airy had the bell rotated a quarter turn so the hammer struck an undamaged spot, a square notch was chipped at the top of the crack to prevent propagation, and a lighter hammer (~4 cwt) was fitted; Big Ben still rings with that crack today. (Sources: UK Parliament "The Great Bell - Big Ben", Whitechapel Bell Foundry archives, Wikipedia "Big Ben")

London's most famous bell cracked two months after it opened because one stubborn man wanted it louder.
It's 1859, and the brand new clock tower over the river is the pride of the whole city.
Workers haul a thirteen-ton bronze giant up the narrow tower, ready to ring out over Westminster.
The makers warn that a bell this big needs a light, gentle hammer.
A normal designer would listen, but Edmund Denison thinks louder is better.
He bolts in an iron monster twice the safe weight, and orders the great clock to strike.
Every hour, the giant hammer slams the rim, rattling hats on the bridge while the crowd cheers.
Then, after two months of strikes, a four-foot crack rips straight up the bronze.
Luckily, the thirteen-ton bell stayed stuck at the top of the tower.
Unluckily, the thirteen-ton bell stayed stuck at the top of the tower.
You see, a bell only rings because bronze bends like a spring and snaps back out.
Ever tapped a glass with a spoon? Each light tap makes it hum, but slam it with a heavy ladle, and the rim splits before it can flex.
That monster iron hammer was hitting the bell harder than the bronze could bend.
Nobody wanted to haul thirteen tons back down, so workers turned the bell a quarter circle, cut a square notch, and fitted a lighter hammer.
So to this day, the most famous chime in the world is actually the sound of a broken bell.
